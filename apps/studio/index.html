Time

A second of simulated time is a second of the exported timeline, always. The step rate says how finely that second is computed and there is one keyframe for every step; the output rate says how finely the Blender timeline is divided. Nothing is resampled between them — a thousand steps into a 60 fps scene puts about seventeen keyframes inside each frame and they are all real, so raising the scene's rate later gives you frames rather than interpolation.

One rendered frame advances the simulation by exactly one output frame, whatever the machine was doing: every step is taken and every step is captured. A slow machine takes longer in wall-clock seconds and produces the identical run. Playback moves one output frame per display refresh, so 60 fps output on a 60 Hz display is life speed and 24 is two and a half times life; neither changes the export. Changing the step rate starts a new run, because the timestep is fixed for the life of one.

Recording

How much the Blender export may hold, per capture — one for the bones and one for the muscle cross-sections, and whichever fills first stops both. Pause before raising it and the capture carries on from where it stopped; raise it while the run is going and capture starts again from that moment, because the ticks in between were never taken and the export promises a keyframe for every one.

The ceiling is this tab's own JavaScript heap rather than the machine's memory: a page is not given the host, and a capture that runs past the heap does not stop politely — the tab dies and takes the run with it. It opens at a fifth of that ceiling because writing the file needs about five copies of the capture live at once, so a bigger budget captures happily and then dies on the Export button. Above the default is yours to judge. A muscle frame is about twenty times a bone frame: a run with the full set costs roughly a hundred and ten kilobytes a tick, so a gigabyte is about nineteen seconds at 500 Hz.

Training

A new name starts a new checkpoint, trained in the scenario on the Scene tab with the body on the Body tab and the joints as the World tab has them. A name that exists is refused unless Resume is ticked, which continues that checkpoint in the scenario it was saved with.

Standing scores every hundredth of a second the head is near its resting height with a foot on the ground, a little for the pelvis staying level and put, a little off for moving and for effort. Balance scores the same moments but by the head: most of the point is lost to a head that is thrown about or turned, so on the tilting floor a body that rides the pulses out with its head still is what wins.

The brain alone starts every muscle slack and leaves the policy to find its own tone. The scenario's script is what the Scene tab plays -- Standing quietly's ankle strategy, say -- and the clip is the tonic posture the first checkpoints were trained over.

A silent body trains a brain with nothing to answer: it picks a posture, holds it, and every episode runs the same, so two candidates score the same and the search cannot rank them. The tremor is a slow random wander on every muscle group, the grain is the error on every sense. Both are drawn from the episode's seed, so a mirrored pair meets the same disturbance and the difference in their scores is the difference in their brains. Zero on both makes a run repeatable.

Recording

Export for Blender writes three files that belong together: the glTF with the bones and the belly mesh, a .pc2 vertex cache the muscles stream from, and the Python script that imports the pair. Keep them in one folder. The muscles are a streamed cache rather than an armature because as armatures they cost 4.3 GB of Blender for six tenths of a second of simulation; as a cache the same import is under 600 MB and a longer run costs disk instead of memory.
