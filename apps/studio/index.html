Time

A second of simulated time is a second of the exported timeline, always. The step rate says how finely that second is computed and there is one keyframe for every step; the output rate says how finely the Blender timeline is divided. Nothing is resampled between them — a thousand steps into a 60 fps scene puts about seventeen keyframes inside each frame and they are all real, so raising the scene's rate later gives you frames rather than interpolation.

One rendered frame advances the simulation by exactly one output frame, whatever the machine was doing: every step is taken and every step is captured. A slow machine takes longer in wall-clock seconds and produces the identical run. While the run is live the picture moves one output frame per display refresh, so 60 fps output on a 60 Hz display is life speed and 24 is two and a half times life; Play on the timeline replays at the output rate in real time. Neither changes the export. A new step rate is used from the next run; the one running keeps its own, because the timestep is fixed for the life of a run. Left alone, the slider follows the body profile's own rate.

Recording

How much the Blender export may hold, per capture — one for the bones and one for the muscle cross-sections, and whichever fills first stops both. Raise it before it fills and the capture carries on. Once it has filled, raising it keeps every frame already held, and they still export; it captures more only on a new run, because the run has gone on past the last captured tick and the export promises a keyframe for every tick it covers. Reset and Start replays the same run, deterministically, with the room to capture more of it.

The ceiling is this tab's own JavaScript heap rather than the machine's memory: a page is not given the host, and a capture that runs past the heap does not stop politely — the tab dies and takes the run with it. It opens at a fifth of that ceiling because writing the file needs about five copies of the capture live at once, so a bigger budget captures happily and then dies on the Export button. Above the default is yours to judge. A muscle frame is many times a bone frame, so with muscles running the muscle capture is the one that fills; the status line above shows how fast.

Spine

The reflexes, under whatever the brain is doing. A muscle pulled past its set point excites itself in proportion to how far and how fast -- the spindle's group II and Ia -- and inhibits its opposite through an interneuron. It needs no training and it is most of what holds a body up: a real person stands on the cord, and the brain only decides where to put the set points. Stretch and Damping both at zero is the body every checkpoint before the cord was trained in.

Stretch is the gain on a strain: how hard a muscle answers being longer than its set point, where 0.1 is a fibre a tenth longer than its own optimal length. Damping is the gain on how fast it is being stretched, and is there to stop the delayed loop ringing. Set point is the length the loop holds the fibre at; zero, its optimal length, keeps the cord quiet until something actually stretches. Reciprocal is how much a muscle's excitation inhibits its opposite. Conduction is the time to the cord and back, about thirty milliseconds in a leg, without which a modelled arc oscillates. The panel opens with no cord, which is the body a run has always opened with; every run starts with what these sliders show. Under the committed standing policy time upright levels off from a stretch of 8.5; see docs/validation/reflex-gains.md.

Training

A new name starts a new checkpoint, trained in the scenario on the Scene tab with the body on the Body tab and the joints as the World tab has them. A name is lower-case letters, digits, dashes and underscores, up to forty, and the panel offers one nothing is called yet. A name that exists is refused unless Resume is ticked. Resume continues the checkpoint's search in whatever the tabs say now, not as it was trained, and the line below lists anything that differs from how it was trained; it continues only the task the checkpoint was trained on. With no dashboard server running, a name the studio ships with is refused, Resume or not: those checkpoints are part of the studio, not this window's to continue, so choose another name.

Standing scores every hundredth of a second the head is near its resting height with a foot on the ground, a little for the pelvis staying level and put, a little off for moving and for effort. Balance scores the same moments but by the head: most of the point is lost to a head that is thrown about or turned, so on the tilting floor a body that rides the pulses out with its head still is what wins.

The brain alone starts every muscle slack and leaves the policy to find its own tone. The scenario's script is what the Scene tab plays -- Standing quietly's ankle strategy, say -- and the clip is the tonic posture the first checkpoints were trained over.

Memory is context the policy carries from one control step to the next: extra senses it reads and extra drives it writes, fed back from its own last answer. Without it the network answers the instant it is shown and nothing else -- it cannot tell a body leaning from one that has leant and come back, cannot average a noisy sense over time, and cannot predict, which is what a nervous system does with the time it spends waiting for its own afferents. Every unit costs a row and a column of weights, and the search's cost grows with the vector it searches.

A silent body trains a brain with nothing to answer: it picks a posture, holds it, and every episode runs the same, so two candidates score the same and the search cannot rank them. The tremor is a slow random wander on every muscle group, the grain is the error on every sense. Both are drawn from the episode's seed, so a mirrored pair meets the same disturbance and the difference in their scores is the difference in their brains. Zero on both makes a run repeatable.

Reference model

The muscles of the model our parameters came from, drawn as paths. Their bones are the models' own meshes, vendored beside the XML. It is a reference and never a target: attachment points here are measured from the Z-Anatomy meshes and stay ours.

A model starts seated on our body: turned by its own change of axes (the legs and the torso are Z-up with the right side on negative X, and the arm is already Y-up and needs only a quarter turn about the vertical), sized by a length both bodies have, and moved so that its anchor joint sits on ours -- the legs by their hip centres, the torso by L5/S1, the arm by the humeral head on our right shoulder. Before a run has started there is no body to sit on, and it waits at our origin. Reset placement goes back to that seat; a placement you have changed is never replaced by it. Each model keeps its own placement. This places the whole model at once, which can never put their shoulder and their hip on ours at the same time -- the proportions differ, and that difference is what is being measured. Use it to get the model roughly on screen; the bone pairing below is what actually registers the two.

Bone pairing

Which of their bones is which of ours. This is what a retarget is built from: a body pair fixes an origin and an orientation, and the joints on that body give it a scale -- a femur that is longer here than there moves every attachment along it. Once a bone is paired, every muscle point carried by it lands on our skeleton instead of theirs.

Retargeted paths are drawn at our body's rest pose, not the live one, because that is the pose both models are comparable in. They will not follow a running body.

Correspondence

Which of their muscles is which of ours. Many of theirs may map to one of ours -- their six external oblique strips are our one external oblique -- so pair as many as belong together. This pairing is the input that measuring the trunk, neck and girdle units against the reference models will need; that measurement is not written yet.

Our points

Joint centres, origins and insertions, and via points on our own skeleton, as handles. Showing points pauses a run and puts the body at rest, because points are defined and recorded at rest; choosing Nothing lets the body go, and the run stays paused until you resume it. The truth here is our mesh, not their model: a point is moved because it is wrong against the bone it sits on. Every move is recorded with the distance, a note and the body it was made on; positions are shown in millimetres.

Recording

Export needs a run: press Start sim.

Export for Blender writes three files that belong together: the glTF with the bones and the belly mesh, a .pc2 vertex cache the muscles stream from, and the Python script that imports the pair. Keep them in one folder. The muscles are a streamed cache rather than an armature because as armatures they cost 4.3 GB of Blender for six tenths of a second of simulation; as a cache the same import is under 600 MB and a longer run costs disk instead of memory.
